This video shows the operation of my newly designed high-density Hall thruster, RAIJIN30, which has a 2.7 times higher propellant mass flow density than our previous thruster, RAIJIN66. It covers the startup and roughly the first minute of operation, with argon as the propellant.

{% include diary-video.html file="2026-11-24_operation" %}

The cathode was already running before the start. We start the thruster by applying the discharge power. After ignition, we increase the magnetic field. In the video you can see the color of the plume change while this happens, until it settles into a stable plume. The camera shows this stable plume as blue, while to the human eye it looks more purple.

**Why does the plasma have a color?**
The light comes from the propellant atoms and ions themselves. Electrons in the plasma collide with them and excite them, and when they relax again they emit light at wavelengths that are characteristic of that gas. The color therefore depends on the gas and on how strongly it is ionized.

- Argon: neutral argon emits mostly in the red and near-infrared, while singly ionized argon has strong lines in the blue. As more of the gas becomes ionized, the glow shifts from reddish-pink towards blue-violet. This is what we see when the magnetic field is increased: the electrons are confined better, which leads to more ionization, and the plume gets bluer.
- Xenon, the usual Hall thruster propellant, glows pale blue to lavender. Neon glows red-orange, and nitrogen pink to violet.
- Camera versus eye: the eye is much less sensitive to deep blue and violet light than a camera sensor, and the camera's color processing shifts the colors further. That is why the same plume looks blue on the video and purple in the room.
